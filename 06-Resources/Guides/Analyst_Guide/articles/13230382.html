As part of our ongoing effort to help clients get the most out of the data collaboration and enhancement capabilities that the Omnisient platform provides we've added SQLPad exports as a way to support insight generation and data analysis.

Protecting customer data sits at the core of everything that we do and our SQLPad export process is designed so that PII never leaves the system, and that export results are strictly limited to non-PII fields, with multiple guardrails in place to support this.

The review and approval process adds a critical human validation layer, giving you final control over how your data generates value in the marketplace. Your approval controls what insights leave your datasets while protecting your data assets and customer privacy.

This guide provides objective criteria to help you approve confidently while protecting your data assets.

The Golden Rule

🥇 If you're unsure about any aspect of an export request: Reject for clarification.

Rejecting an export is not always a denial, it's a request for more information. The data buyer can revise their query or our support team can assist.

It is always safer to reject and clarify than to approve something you don't fully understand.

This protects both you and the data buyer: you maintain control over your data asset and they receive only compliant data exports that they can confidently use.

Understanding What You're Reviewing

What the Platform Already Blocks (You Won't See These)

These are automatically restricted and cannot appear in export requests:

Term

Definition

PII (Personally Identifiable Information)

Information that can directly identify an individual, such as names, email addresses, phone numbers, or government-issued identifiers. PII is never allowed to leave the platform.

Hashed Data

Data that has been transformed into a fixed-length string using a mathematical process. Hashing is often used to protect sensitive values, but hashed data can still behave like an identifier and is therefore restricted.

What You're Reviewing (Allowed if Appropriate)

Term

Definition

Aggregation

A method of summarising data (for example, counts or averages) rather than showing individual records.

Non-PII Data

Information that does not directly identify an individual. SQLPad exports are limited to non-PII fields.

Red Flags to Watch For (Obfuscation Techniques)

Term

Definition

Column Aliasing

The practice of renaming a column when preparing data for export. This is commonly done to make data easier to read, but it can also make it less clear what the original field represents if the name is very generic or unexpected.

Identifiers

A value used to distinguish or reference a specific record, such as a key or reference number. Identifiers are often consistent in format and may appear sequential or structured.

SKEYS

A system-generated key used to link data across internal tables. SKEY values are not visible within SQLPad but may appear as references in exports under certain conditions.

DW Keys

A data warehouse key used internally to reference records. DW keys are identifiers and are restricted from being exported directly.

💡 Your Job

Verify that what buyers are requesting matches the category they claim, and watch for attempts to disguise restricted data as allowed data.

Awareness Tips for Reviewing Export Samples

While SQLPad exports has been designed with strong safeguards to prevent sensitive information from ever being exported, it’s still helpful to build familiarity with how data may appear in different formats. Occasionally, values might be presented in ways that are unexpected or unfamiliar, simply because of how users structure their queries or label their columns.

By understanding these patterns, you’ll feel more confident when reviewing a sample and recognizing when something may need a second look or clarification. This section highlights a few indicators that may prompt you to pause and double-check.

1. Column Names and Labels

Column names provide the first layer of context when reviewing a dataset. In most cases, they clearly describe the information being shared. It can be helpful to pause and ensure the names align with the dataset and its intended purpose.

Things to consider:

  • Do the column names make sense for this dataset?
    For example, would it be reasonable for a demographics export to include a column related to revenue or transactions?

  • Are any column names very generic (e.g. value, info, result) and therefore unclear about what they represent?

  • Do any column names reference inherently sensitive concepts (such as salary or credit-related terms) that might benefit from additional context?

  • Are there minor spelling variations or typos that make the meaning less clear?

Clear and well-aligned naming generally makes review easier. If a name feels unclear, a quick clarification can help ensure shared understanding.

2. How the Data Behaves

Beyond the column name, the values themselves can provide useful context. Looking at how data behaves across rows often helps confirm whether it aligns with what the label suggests.

Things to consider:

  • Do the values vary in a way you would normally expect for this type of data?

  • Are numbers unusually consistent, sequential, or uniformly structured?

  • Do text values appear to follow a fixed pattern or length across all rows?

3. Formatting and Presentation

Formatting choices can sometimes make data easier to read and sometimes make it harder to interpret at a glance.

Things to consider:

  • Are currency symbols, prefixes, or units applied consistently?

  • Do decimals, number formats, or text styles align with what you would expect?

  • Are there mixed formats within the same column (e.g. numbers and text together)?

If formatting makes a column harder to interpret, it’s reasonable to pause and ask for clarification.

4. Dataset Context and Intended Use

Stepping back and considering the dataset as a whole can be one of the most helpful review techniques.

Things to consider:

  • Does the sample feel consistent with the type of information being shared?

  • Does it align with what the data partner requested and would reasonably need?

  • Would the dataset still make sense if one or two column names were changed?

💡 This broader context check often helps confirm whether everything fits together as expected.

Examples: What to Approve vs. Reject

Scenario 1: Rejected - Disguised Identifiers

A financial services company requests "customer segmentation data" for marketing purposes.

Why This Fails

Criteria

Issue

Column Names

Generic and vague. "value_bracket" and "activity_score" don't clearly describe what they measure.

Suspicious Patterns

Sequential numbering in "customer_type" (10001, 10002, 10003, 10004) suggests individual identifiers.

High Cardinality

Each row appears unique, this suggests data that's not truly aggregated.

Obfuscation

Column aliasing hides the true meaning. "value_bracket" could be income and "activity_score" could be a customer ID.

Reviewer Decision: ❌ Reject

Rejection Reason: "This export contains sequential identifiers (PREM_10001, 10002...) rather than aggregated segments. Please revise to show true customer categories (e.g., 'Premium Customers', 'Standard Customers') with counts or percentages rather than individual customer records."

Scenario 2: Rejected - Highly Granular Aggregations

A retailer requests "aggregated shopping behavior insights" across multiple dimensions.

Why This Fails

Criteria

Issue

Granularity

Five dimensions combined create micro-segments of 1-3 customers each.

Re-identification Risk

Highly specific combinations could identify individuals e.g., "23-24 year old in Sandton earning 45-47K buying luxury beauty in March".

Pseudo-aggregation

Claims to be "aggregated" but group sizes are so small they're essentially individual records.

Privacy Violation

Combined attributes create unique or near-unique fingerprints.

Reviewer Decision: ❌ Reject

Rejection Reason: "This aggregation is too granular, creating segments of 1-3 customers that could enable re-identification. Please reduce dimensions (e.g., broader age groups: '20-30', larger geographic areas: 'Northern Suburbs') or focus on fewer combined attributes to ensure meaningful group sizes of 10+ customers."

Scenario 3: Rejected - Mismatched Columns and Values

A telecommunications company requests "customer demographic insights" for market research.

Why This Fails

Criteria

Issue

Value Mismatch

"customer_segment" shows sequential numbers instead of meaningful categories.

Disguised Identifiers

Numbers 8847392, 8847396 etc. appear to be system-generated customer IDs.

Inconsistent Data Types

"customer_segment" suggests categorical segments but contains numerical identifiers.

Pattern Recognition

Sequential numbering is a clear red flag for individual identifiers

Reviewer Decision: ❌ Reject

Rejection Reason: "The 'customer_segment' column contains sequential numerical values (8847392, 8847393...) that appear to be customer identifiers rather than segment names. Please replace with descriptive segment labels like 'High Value', 'Regular User', 'Occasional Customer' etc."

Scenario 4: Approved - Proper Aggregated Insights

A bank requests customer behavior analysis for product development and marketing strategy.

Why This Passes

Criteria

Issue

Clear Column Names

All columns clearly describe what they measure: age_group, account_type, etc.

Appropriate Granularity

Age ranges are broad (7-14 year spans), spending in ranges, not exact amounts.

Meaningful Groups

Customer counts range from 445-2,156 - true aggregation with substantial group sizes

No Identifiers

No sequential numbering or unique values that could identify individuals.

Logical Categories

Account types and frequency bands use descriptive labels.

Business Relevance

Data aligns with data-buyers purpose of product development.

Reviewer Decision: ✅ Approved

This export demonstrates proper aggregation with meaningful customer segments, appropriate group sizes, and clear business utility without privacy risks.

Support & Escalation

If you need support or feel unsure about an export, don’t hesitate to reach out to the support team. The requester’s email is included in the export, so you may also contact them directly if you’d like additional clarity or context.

Reject an export directly if:

  • The issue is clear (disguised identifiers, mismatched columns, vague naming).

  • Your rejection reason can guide the buyer to fix it.

  • This is a standard case covered by the guide.

Escalate to support if:

  • You suspect intentional policy violation or repeated problematic requests from the same buyer.

  • Technical question about data formats you can't interpret.

  • Disagreement with a buyer after an export is rejected and clarification has been attempted.

  • An urgent business need that requires faster resolution than a standard rejection/resubmission.

What to Include in Your Escalation:

  • Project code and creation date from the export request.

  • Screenshot highlighting the specific concern.

  • What you've already tried.

  • Why this needs escalation (e.g., "Buyer claims urgent business deadline").

What Buyers See

Understanding what guidance buyers receive helps you provide better feedback:

Buyers are told the following:

  • "Omnisient permits only the download of aggregated results".

  • They must confirm their export "does not contain information that could lead to the identification of any individual".

  • Their request will be reviewed by data partners and Omnisient.

What buyers don't automatically see:

  • Specific examples of what qualifies as "aggregated".

  • The red flags you're checking for (sequential IDs, obfuscation, etc.)

Your rejection feedback is often the first time a buyer learns what's actually required. Ensure your rejection reason is educational:

Not helpful: "This doesn't look aggregated"

Helpful: "This export contains fields with sequential values (Customer_Type: CUST_10001, CUST_10002...) which appear to be identifiers rather than aggregated categories. Please revise your query to group data into meaningful segments (e.g., customer types: 'Frequent Buyer', 'Occasional', 'New') rather than exporting individual customer records."

Maintaining Consistency Across Your Review Team

If multiple people at your organization review SQLPad exports:

Best Practices

  1. Designate a lead reviewer who makes final calls on edge cases.

  2. Review first 5-10 exports together to calibrate standards.

  3. Document your decisions. Keep internal notes on why specific exports were approved/rejected to build institutional knowledge.

  4. Weekly sync (15 min) to discuss any unclear cases and align on standards.

  5. Share this guide with all reviewers and confirm everyone understands the "reject if unsure" principle.

If Reviewers Disagree

  • Default to the MORE CAUTIOUS position: rejection.

  • Escalate to your lead reviewer or our support team.

  • Never approve if any reviewer has unresolved concerns.

Building Your Review Library

Consider keeping screenshots of:

  • Good examples you've approved (reference for future).

  • Bad examples you've rejected (training for new reviewers).

  • Edge cases you escalated (learn from support's guidance).

Conclusion

Reviewing SQLPad exports doesn’t need to feel complex or intimidating. With a small amount of attention and familiarity, most samples can be assessed quickly and confidently. Paying thoughtful attention to column names, formatting, and overall consistency helps ensure the data aligns with what was intended to be shared.

If something doesn’t immediately make sense, it’s perfectly reasonable to pause, seek clarification, or make use of the Support & Escalation guidance.

By taking a considered approach and using the tools and guidance available, you play an important role in maintaining consistent, well-reviewed data sharing.
